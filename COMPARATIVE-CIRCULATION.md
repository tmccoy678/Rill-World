# Comparative circulation inspirations

## Purpose

No animal circulation is universally best. Each lineage solves transport under
different constraints of scale, pressure, temperature, oxygen availability,
water balance, and activity. Rillworld should preserve several distinct
solutions as inspiration without installing any named biological role into the
candidate physics.

## Human mammalian pattern: deformation through single-file passages

Human red blood cells are wider than the smallest capillary lumens. They deform
substantially, travel single-file, preserve their membrane connectivity and
cargo, and recover after leaving the constriction.

The important properties are functional rather than visual:

- no central controller;
- a flexible connected boundary;
- high carried capacity;
- unconstrained width larger than the narrow passage;
- identity preserved through major geometric deformation;
- close interaction with the passage wall and surrounding plasma;
- maturation, aging, and eventual clearance.

This is the reference for a large deformable Rill carrier.

## Camelid pattern: small, oval, and shape-stable

Camelid red cells are small and elliptical. Their narrow dimension permits
capillary passage with less deformation, while their membrane organization
supports extreme dehydration and rapid rehydration.

This suggests a distinct carrier lineage: smaller cargo, durable geometry, and
environmental resilience rather than maximal flexibility.

## Mouse-deer pattern: reduce the carrier scale

Very small mammalian red cells show that carrier size itself can vary greatly.
A smaller carrier may carry less per unit while permitting finer passages,
higher counts, and less deformation.

Rillworld should not assume one canonical mature carrier size.

## Cephalopod pattern: closed circulation with distributed pumping

Cephalopods combine a closed, branching vascular network with multiple pumps.
Two branchial hearts support flow through the gills while a systemic heart
supplies the rest of the body. Their oxygen carrier is dissolved in the fluid
rather than packaged into red cells.

This contributes two principles:

- circulation may be maintained by several regional oscillators rather than
  one central pump;
- essential small material may remain dissolved in the medium and continue
  moving around larger carriers or blockages.

## Antarctic icefish pattern: medium-first, low-resistance circulation

Adult channichthyid icefish lack hemoglobin and red blood cells. Oxygen travels
primarily dissolved in plasma. They compensate through low viscosity, high
blood volume, enlarged hearts, high output, extensive vasculature, large-bore
capillaries, and dense downstream mitochondrial structure.

This demonstrates that a carrier-poor system may remain viable when the medium
itself transports sparse resources and circulation volume is high.

## Combined design lessons

The future Rill medium should permit at least three scales of transport:

1. dissolved local states that move around larger structures;
2. small shape-stable carriers that pass narrow regions readily;
3. larger deformable carriers that move single-file through constrained
   passages.

The smallest passage does not need massive internal parallelism. It may admit
one large carrier while dissolved states continue through the surrounding
medium. Whole-body scale arises from an enormous branching network of locally
serial passages.

Blockage may create upstream pressure and downstream scarcity. Possible local
consequences include deformation, rerouting, fragmentation, decay, passage-wall
change, sealing, and reopening. If local structures eventually restore the
conditions that maintain circulation, the network approaches self-maintenance.
